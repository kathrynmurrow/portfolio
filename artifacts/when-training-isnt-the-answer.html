Case Study
When Training Isn't the Answer
How a multi-unit restaurant brand built a leadership development system on an undiagnosed foundation — and what a diagnostic-first approach would have revealed before the first course was ever built.

The Setup

Two years ago, I was brought in as a consultant to a growing multi-unit QSR brand to assess their leadership development infrastructure. Six months later, they built a Director-level role around my findings and hired me into it. The mandate was clear: build a scalable leadership development function from the ground up.

Over the next eighteen months, I did exactly that. I designed and built a four-course Leadership Foundations Series — Own the Shift, Lead the Business, Own the Business, and Leading the Leaders — covering every field leadership role from Shift Leader to Area Director. I built a Facilitator Certification System — a structured 3-session professional development program for our internal team — architected the LMS ecosystem, and integrated AI-powered course generation through a multi-agent system I developed called CourseForge. By any measurable output standard, it was a significant body of work.

And somewhere in the middle of building it, I started asking a question I couldn't stop asking:

"What if the problem we're solving isn't the problem that actually exists?"

The question that changed how I think about this work

What the Organization Believed

The organizational theory of change, as I understood it, looked like this: field leaders have performance gaps → those gaps exist because leaders lack knowledge and skills → if we give them structured learning experiences, performance will improve. Build the courses. Fill the gaps. Move on.

This is a reasonable theory. It's also the default theory in most organizations — and it deserves scrutiny before it becomes a multi-year investment.

The signal that something else was happening came not from a single dramatic failure, but from a pattern of smaller observations over time. Facilitator feedback from the two GM cohorts we ran was strong — leaders found the content relevant, the sessions engaging, the frameworks applicable. On paper, the program was working.

But when I looked at what happened after leaders left the room, the picture changed. There was no structured reinforcement. No field follow-up protocol. No mechanism connecting what was learned in a two-day session to what was expected on a Tuesday morning six weeks later. The organization's own directive — "getting them in the classes is more important than having the entire system built" — told me everything I needed to know about the theory of change driving the work.

Speed over system. Presence over transfer. Activity over impact.

What a Diagnosis Would Have Revealed

Using the diagnostic framework I subsequently developed — a ten-section organizational problem analysis grounded in root cause methodology, stakeholder incentive mapping, and change readiness assessment — here is what a rigorous pre-build diagnosis would have surfaced.

The Cascade Failure — Same Problem at Every Level
1
VP of Operations / Regional Leadership
The leadership standard exists on paper. It is not modeled or enforced consistently at the top of the field hierarchy. When the people responsible for holding the standard don't visibly live it, every layer below receives a conflicting signal.
2
Regional Directors of Operations
The most critical coaching layer in the field has no formal development infrastructure. RDOs are expected to develop Area Directors — but have received no structured investment in their own capability to do so. This gap is invisible in the current development strategy.
3
Area Directors
ADs are being asked to develop GMs using a standard their own supervisors aren't consistently applying. Coaching quality is entirely dependent on individual manager capability — with no shared model, no reinforcement structure, and no accountability mechanism above them.
4
GMs, AGMs, Shift Leaders
Field leaders attend development programs and return to environments that do not reinforce what was learned. The class becomes an island. Transfer does not occur — not because the learning was poor, but because the environment does not support it.
5
The Course Catalog
Presented as the solution to performance gaps that are structural, not instructional. Building more courses without addressing the cascade above them does not close the gap — it adds cost and activity to a system whose conditions for success haven't been established.

Three Compounding Problems

1
A modeling and accountability gap at the top
The standard exists. It is not lived consistently by the people responsible for holding it. This is not a training problem — it is a leadership alignment and performance management problem. Thomas Gilbert's Behavioral Engineering Model suggests that environmental factors like this account for approximately 80% of performance problems. Knowledge and skill interventions — training — account for the remaining 20%.
2
An onboarding velocity problem
Leaders are being moved into roles faster than the organization can prepare them. High turnover and rapid hiring create constant pressure to fill seats before building capability. A 3-to-4 hour class cannot compress what someone needs 60 to 90 days to absorb — and when the organization's posture is speed over readiness, no amount of curriculum design compensates.
3
A coaching infrastructure problem
Development in this organization is event-based — a class, a workshop — rather than embedded in daily operations. The people responsible for developing others in the field don't consistently have the skills, the time, or the accountability structures to do it. Regional Directors of Operations — the linchpin of the entire field model — have no development pathway at all.

What Was Built Anyway — and Why It Still Matters

The Leadership Foundations Series is good work. The courses are well-designed, competency-mapped, and field-grounded. The Facilitator Certification System is a rigorous, well-designed professional development program. The AI-powered generation infrastructure behind it is genuinely novel. None of that is the problem.

The problem is the absence of the diagnostic layer that should have preceded it — and the absence of the measurement layer that should be running alongside it. Good solutions deployed against the wrong diagnosis produce activity, not outcomes.

The Honest Assessment

Thirty-eight GM-level leaders have completed the foundational course. Thirty-one submitted a feedback survey — meaning we have reaction data from 82% of participants, and none at all from the remaining 18%. Participant feedback from those who responded is strong. We do not yet know whether it is changing behavior in the field. We do not have a measurement system connecting course completion to operational KPIs. We have not yet run the transfer audit that would tell us whether the environment supports what the classroom is trying to build. The organization's current posture — expand the catalog before validating the foundation — is the pattern this case study is designed to interrogate.

The Diagnostic Approach

In response to these observations, I developed a structured diagnostic framework and facilitation methodology designed to surface organizational problems before solutions are designed. The core argument is simple: organizations consistently jump to solutions — including training — before fully understanding the problem they're solving. The cost of that pattern is not just wasted resources. It is the erosion of trust in the function that built the solution.

The framework operates across ten diagnostic sections, moving from goal clarity through gap analysis, historical context, stakeholder mapping, change readiness assessment, resource inventory, process evaluation, external forces, measurement design, and prioritization. It is designed to be used as a pre-work instrument — with independent submissions from all involved parties before any group conversation occurs.

What the Diagnostic Would Recommend for This Organization
  • Pause catalog expansion until a transfer audit on the existing foundational series establishes whether the conditions for learning transfer exist in the field.
  • Design and deploy an RDO development pathway — the most urgent and most defensible gap in the current system, and the one most likely to produce downstream impact.
  • Run a field leadership alignment diagnostic with VP of Ops, RDOs, and Area Directors focused on shared mental models, incentive alignment, and coaching accountability.
  • Build the measurement infrastructure before the next cohort launches — connecting leading indicators to lagging indicators like turnover, audit performance, and internal promotion rate.
  • Reframe the L&D function's mandate from course delivery to organizational diagnosis and targeted intervention.

What This Case Is Really About

This is not a case study about a failed training program. The program hasn't failed — it hasn't been fully tested yet. This is a case study about what happens when a function is asked to build solutions faster than the organization is willing to understand problems.

The most important skill in organizational effectiveness is not the ability to design good programs. It is the ability to diagnose whether a program is the right intervention in the first place — and to make that case clearly, with evidence, before resources are committed.

That belief is what I bring to every engagement. It is also what this case study is evidence of.

Strategic Recommendation
Before We Go Further
A research-informed case for why expanding facilitator certification to Area Directors and franchise training managers — before validating what's already been built — is the wrong next move, and what the right sequence actually is.

The Situation

We have built something real. Four leadership courses. A Facilitator Certification System — a 3-session, 6-month professional development program for our internal team. A multi-agent AI system that generates full course suites. LMS architecture. Data dashboards. Across two years, this function has produced a meaningful body of work — and 100 people have been through our programs.

The organization's current direction is to continue expanding. The immediate ask is to certify Area Directors and franchise training managers as facilitators this year — before the existing system has been evaluated, before the transfer infrastructure exists, and before there is a single piece of evidence showing that what has already been built is changing behavior in the field.

This document makes the case that doing so is the wrong move — not because the programs aren't good, but because deploying an unvalidated system at greater scale and lower trainer readiness does not multiply impact. It multiplies risk.

What the Research Says

The transfer of training literature gives us clear answers about what makes training work and what makes it fail. The conditions currently in place match the failure profile with precision.

📉
Baldwin & Ford (1988) — Personnel Psychology
Only ~10% of training investment results in sustained behavior change on the job.
The most cited statistic in training research. The primary reason is not program quality — it is the absence of environmental conditions that support transfer: supervisor reinforcement, accountability for application, and organizational climate that rewards new behaviors.
Baldwin, T. T., & Ford, J. K. (1988). Personnel Psychology, 41, 63–105.
👤
Blume, Ford, Baldwin & Huang (2010) — Journal of Management
Supervisor support is one of the strongest predictors of whether training transfers to the job.
A meta-analysis of 89 empirical studies confirmed that learners whose supervisors encourage, support, and monitor learning transfer are significantly more likely to apply what they learned. When supervisors don't reinforce, transfer doesn't happen — regardless of program quality.
Blume et al. (2010). Journal of Management, 36(4), 1065–1105.
🔁
Kirkpatrick & Kirkpatrick (2016) — New World Kirkpatrick Model
Training should be designed backward from Level 4 results — not built and evaluated later.
The 2016 update introduced "Required Drivers" — workplace reinforcement systems that must exist for behavior change to occur. These are prerequisite conditions, not additions. Without them, even well-designed training produces reaction data, not results.
Kirkpatrick, J. D., & Kirkpatrick, W. K. (2016). Kirkpatrick's Four Levels. ATD Press.
⚙️
Burke & Hutchins (2007) — Human Resource Development Review
Transfer climate is the most consistent predictor of whether training produces behavioral change.
An integrative literature review found that transfer climate, supervisor support, and opportunity to perform are more predictive of training outcomes than training design itself. An organization can build a perfect program and still produce no behavioral change if the climate doesn't support application.
Burke, L. A., & Hutchins, H. M. (2007). Human Resource Development Review, 6(3), 263–296.

Strategic Thinking
An Honest Evaluation
Thinking through what we're doing, why we're doing it, what the data actually shows, and what I genuinely believe the right path forward is — with my own bias named and set aside.
Why This Document Exists
I have a bias. I built these programs. I believe in them. And I am aware that both of those things can make it harder to evaluate them honestly. This document is my attempt to do that anyway — to ask the hard questions, name what I don't know, acknowledge what could be working even if I can't prove it, and be clear about what I believe the data is actually telling us. The goal is not to tear down what exists. It is to understand what it will take to make it actually work.

What We've Built and Why It Matters

Over two years, this function has produced four leadership courses, a Facilitator Certification System for our internal team, a multi-agent AI course generation system, LMS architecture, and data dashboards that surface operational visibility leadership didn't previously have. One hundred people have been through our programs. Participant feedback is strong — satisfaction scores averaging 4.72 out of 5.

I want to be clear that I believe this work is good. The courses are well-designed, competency-mapped, and field-grounded. Participants say so consistently and in their own words. That is real evidence, and I am not dismissing it.

But I have been sitting with a harder question: good content delivered into a system that can't support its transfer is not enough. And right now, I don't think we have the system.

The Tension I'm Holding

I've been genuinely trying to argue both sides of this. Here is what the honest case for and against our current direction actually looks like.

The Case For Continuing As-Is
Arguments I take seriously and cannot dismiss
  • Mandating quarterly sessions creates organizational commitment — without it, the program disappears under operational pressure.
  • Visible, consistent investment signals to field leaders that the organization values their growth.
  • Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. We simply haven't measured it yet.
  • The content is genuinely relevant. Participants leave with applicable frameworks.
  • Stopping now would create cynicism that would follow any future initiative.
My Honest Concerns
What I can't argue away
  • We have no evidence — zero — that what is taught is changing how anyone does their job.
  • The measure of success is a delivery schedule. That measures inputs, not outcomes.
  • Every condition the research identifies as necessary for training transfer is currently absent.
  • The organizational posture — "getting them in the classes is more important" — prioritizes activity over impact by design.
  • We are being asked to expand before validating. That is not how effective systems are built.

"We have strong evidence that people enjoyed the experience and intended to apply what they learned. We have no evidence of whether any of it changed how anyone does their job."

The honest summary of our data position — across all four initiatives

The Question I Keep Asking

I've been working through a chain of questions to understand whether my concern is legitimate or whether I'm letting my bias toward rigor get in the way. Here is that chain, answered as honestly as I can.

Q
Are the programs good?
Yes. Content is relevant, facilitator quality is strong, participant engagement is high. The design is sound.
Q
Are participants learning?
We believe so, based on self-reported confidence gains. We have no formal assessment data confirming knowledge or skill acquisition (Level 2).
Q
Are participants applying what they learned in the field?
We do not know. There is no follow-up mechanism, no behavioral observation data, no supervisor confirmation process. This is the critical gap.
Q
Is the program moving KPIs?
Unknown. No KPI linkage exists. We cannot answer this question with current systems.
Q
Could we track this if we tried?
This is where the real barrier lives. Manager walks are tracked on laminated paper that gets erased with each use. No digital record. No persistent data. A GM can say they did one and no one would ever know they didn't. We have no reliable operational infrastructure to verify whether the field behaviors training is designed to produce are actually happening.
Q
Is inconsistent data better than no data?
Marginally — but not meaningfully for decision-making. What inconsistent data can do is document the gap itself — and that documentation is evidence for why the infrastructure problem must be named and owned before the program expands.
Q
Is this an L&D problem or an operational infrastructure problem?
Both — but not equally. The field behavior tracking gap is an operational problem that predates this function. The evaluation gap — our failure to build L2–L4 measurement into program architecture — is an L&D problem we own. Both must be named. Only one is ours to fix.

Three Problems, Three Levels of Ownership

The problems compounding each other sit at different levels of the organization. Conflating them — treating them all as one "training problem" — is what leads to solutions that don't fit.

Layer 1
Structural
Owned by Operations & Executive Leadership
No consistent model of leadership behavior from the top down
VP of Ops and RDOs are not consistently modeling or enforcing the standard the courses are teaching. When the people responsible for holding the standard don't visibly live it, the signal to every layer below is that the standard is optional. No course corrects for this.
Layer 2
Operational
Owned by Operations
No field behavior tracking infrastructure exists
Manager walks are recorded on laminated paper that is erased after each use. There is no persistent, verifiable record of whether coached field behaviors are happening. This makes it structurally impossible for L&D to demonstrate training impact. The absence of field behavior data is a documented operational finding, not an evaluation failure.
Layer 3
L&D
Owned by this function
Programs were built without evaluation infrastructure or transfer architecture
Courses were designed and delivered without Level 2–4 measurement, post-session follow-up protocols, supervisor briefing systems, or behavioral accountability mechanisms. This is what we own. It does not mean the programs failed. It means we cannot prove they succeeded.
The Critical Implication
Training cannot fix Layer 1 or Layer 2. But expanding training into environments where Layer 1 and Layer 2 haven't been addressed will produce more activity with the same unmeasurable impact — at greater cost. The ask to certify Area Directors and franchise managers puts facilitation responsibility into Layer 1 — the very layer where the standard isn't being consistently modeled. That is a risk multiplier, not a force multiplier.

The First Honest Conversation

We Haven't Diagnosed the Problem. We've Assumed It.

Thomas Gilbert's Behavioral Engineering Model — validated empirically in 2010 by Crossman in Performance Improvement Quarterly and replicated in large-scale organizational studies through 2024 — establishes that approximately 75–80% of performance problems are environmental, not individual. Deming estimated 94% belong to the system. Mager and Pipe's diagnostic framework starts with a single question: "Could they do it if their lives depended on it?" If yes, training is not the answer. Our programs may be good. But we have not yet asked whether training is the right intervention for the performance gaps we're trying to close.

The Research-Backed Diagnostic Question
Before designing or expanding any program, three questions must be answered: (1) Is the gap a knowledge/skill deficit, or is it environmental — unclear expectations, missing tools, misaligned incentives, no accountability structure? (2) If environmental, which layer owns it — executive leadership, operations, or L&D? (3) Are the conditions that make training transfer possible actually present in the field? All three are currently unanswered for our programs.
Layer 1 · Structural
Is the standard being modeled at the top?
When leaders responsible for holding the standard don't consistently model it, every layer below receives a conflicting signal. No training program corrects for this. This is owned by executive leadership and operations — not L&D.
Layer 2 · Operational
Can we even verify what's happening in the field?
Field behaviors tracked on erasable paper with no persistent record make it structurally impossible to demonstrate training impact. This is a documented operational barrier — not an L&D evaluation failure. Owned by operations.
Layer 3 · L&D
Did we build in the scaffolding that makes learning stick?
Programs were built without Level 2–4 measurement, post-session follow-up, supervisor briefing protocols, or behavioral accountability mechanisms. This is what L&D owns. It doesn't mean programs failed. It means we can't prove they succeeded.
The Harder Conversation

Our Current Measurement Tools Cannot Close the Loop

This is the conversation that gets skipped most often — and skipping it is precisely why organizations keep running programs they can't validate. Our current measurement relies on two tools: self-reported surveys and observation during store visits. Both have documented, quantified limitations that directly affect our ability to know whether training is working.

Problem 1 · Self-Report Bias
"Feel more equipped" doesn't mean they are more equipped.
Sitzmann et al.'s (2010) landmark meta-analysis in the Academy of Management Learning & Education found self-assessment correlated with motivation (r = .59) but actual cognitive learning at only r = .14–.28. Approximately 30% of evaluation studies misinterpret self-reported confidence as evidence of learning. Our post-session surveys measure how people felt about the experience. They do not measure whether learning occurred — and they do not measure whether behavior changed.
Problem 2 · Observation Inflation
Compliance during audits is not compliance on a Tuesday morning.
The Hawthorne effect in operational compliance contexts is not minor. Eckmanns et al. (2006) measured a 55% compliance inflation during overt observation in high-accountability environments. Srigley et al. (2014, BMJ Quality & Safety) documented a 253% inflation comparing observed vs. electronically monitored behavior. If we use store visits or manager walks to verify whether trained behaviors are happening in the field, we are measuring performance theater — not real behavior.
Problem 3 · Paper Tracking Error Rate
Erasable paper produces no persistent, verifiable record.
Nahm et al. (2008, PLOS ONE) found paper-to-database workflows produce approximately 976 errors per 10,000 fields; electronic systems produce 14.3 — a 68-fold difference. But the deeper problem is not error rate. It is that a manager can say a coached behavior happened and no record exists to verify or contradict it. We have a structural data gap, not a data quality problem. The gap must be named as a business case before L&D can make any defensible claims about training ROI.
Problem 4 · The Scrap Learning Reality
Most of what we've delivered likely hasn't transferred.
Brinkerhoff's Success Case Method research (2003/2005) found that in a typical training program, approximately 15–20% of participants get meaningful results. The remaining 80% is "scrap learning" — content that was delivered but never applied. ATD's 2025 benchmarking data confirms: only 35% of organizations consistently evaluate at Level 4, and 87% find it difficult to isolate training impact. We are not an outlier. We are in the majority. The question is whether we're willing to change that.
The Candid Bottom Line

We currently have strong evidence that people enjoyed our programs and intended to apply what they learned. We have no credible evidence that any of it changed how anyone does their job. This is not a failure of program design. It is a failure of infrastructure — measurement, reinforcement, and accountability structures that should have been built before the first cohort launched. The good news: this is fixable. The research on what works is clear. What follows is the sequence that the evidence supports.

Build the 30/60/90-Day Follow-Up Architecture
Design the behavioral check-in system that closes the loop between classroom and field. This is the mechanism that produces Level 3 data.

Sample 30-Day Check-In Questions (Behaviorally Specific)

AI Automation — Built Into the System
These are not experimental uses of AI. They are specific, repeatable automation tasks that reduce L&D manual work while increasing the speed and quality of data synthesis, reporting, and follow-up.
📋
Diagnostic Synthesis Engine
After Week 2 submissions close: all ten-section responses pasted into Claude. Outputs a structured facilitator brief identifying consensus points, divergence areas, blind spots, and an opening question for the group session. Runs in under 10 minutes.
Tool: Claude (Anthropic) · Prompt: pre-built synthesis template
📊
Check-In Thematic Coder
After each check-in wave closes: AI codes all open-text responses against Gilbert's BEM domains (information, resources, incentives, knowledge, capacity, motivation). Produces a ranked list of the top barriers by domain. Drives the conversation with operations about what's blocking transfer.
Tool: Claude API or ChatGPT · Batch processing via Apps Script
📄
Report Generator
Monthly and quarterly: AI generates first draft of all stakeholder reports from raw data inputs. L&D reviews, edits, and frames. Cuts report production time from ~4 hours to ~45 minutes. Maintains consistent format and language across all reporting periods.
Tool: Claude or ChatGPT · Template-driven generation
⚡
CourseForge — Transfer Tool Generator
Phase 2 tool builds: input program content + behavior map → CourseForge generates first drafts of all three AD briefing tools (pre-brief, mid-pulse, post-reinforcement guide). Eliminates blank-page problem and ensures consistency across programs.
Tool: CourseForge (custom multi-agent system) · Job ticket format
🔍
Comparative Cohort Analyzer
Phase 4: bi-weekly comparison of Level 3 application rates across certified vs. non-certified AD cohorts. AI flags statistically meaningful differences (>10 percentage points) and generates a plain-language explanation of what the difference suggests about facilitator impact.
Tool: Claude API · Structured data input via Google Sheets or LMS export
🚨
At-Risk Cohort Flagging
Phase 4 maturity: AI analyzes early check-in signals (30-day low response rate, high barrier naming, low application self-report) to flag cohorts likely to show poor 90-day transfer. L&D receives an alert within 48 hours of the 30-day window closing, enabling proactive outreach before the 90-day window.
Tool: Claude API · Trigger: automated email alert to L&D lead

Before You Refine This

This is a draft. The week-by-week tasks, KPI targets, and role assignments are starting points — not mandates. Review it against three questions before embedding it in the portfolio:

1. What's realistic given current capacity? Some phases may need to be extended. Some tasks may need to be simplified. Adjust without losing the core logic: diagnose before building, build before expanding, expand before scaling.

2. What needs to be added for your specific context? Franchise-specific constraints, existing digital tools, HR system capabilities — none of this is in the draft because none of it was known at the time of writing.

3. What would you cut if you had to? If resources are constrained, the non-negotiables are: the Phase 1 diagnostic, the 30/60-day check-ins, and the operational tracking business case. Everything else is valuable but adjustable.
