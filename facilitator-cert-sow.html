The diagnosis

This series needed to become a program, not a collection of events.

Three good training days don't make a development program.

Round 1 of the certification series revealed a core structural problem: the three sessions were individually sound but functionally disconnected. No pre-work, no between-session accountability, no shared reflection artifact across the series, no defined standard for what "certified" actually means. Participants couldn't track their own growth because there was no longitudinal thread.

The redesign treats the six months between Session 1 and the certification decision as the program — not the three days. The sessions are the anchors. The work between them is where development happens.

This same diagnosis-before-solution principle is the foundation of the WiRL keynote framework — Teach, Coach, Measure — and it applies here directly: before redesigning any session, the gaps had to be named specifically.

Design decisions

What stays. What goes. What changes.

Each decision tied to a specific gap identified in Round 1. Nothing cut without a reason. Nothing added without a purpose.

Cuts — removing what isn't earning its time
Cut

Excess group-sort transitions

Candy groups, famous quad squads, Nickelodeon characters, iconic art — these mechanics appear 3–4 times per session and create repeated momentum breaks without instructional payoff.

Why it mattersAdult learners, especially experienced field leaders, disengage when regrouping activities feel like filler. Limit to one per session maximum, and only when it reinforces content.
Cut

Sewing role titles in Session 3

"Seam Ripper," "Pattern Keeper," "Question Threader" — the metaphor adds cognitive load for participants who are simultaneously managing unfamiliar facilitation skills.

Why it mattersFor participants new to facilitating, every layer of abstraction is a barrier. Replace with plain language: Timekeeper, Engagement Lead, Facilitator, Question Crafter.
Cut

Standalone goal-setting without follow-through

Session 2 has participants write two facilitator goals that never resurface in Session 3 or any checkpoint. Creates the appearance of accountability without the structure to support it.

Why it mattersGoals without a mechanism — a check-in, a revisit, a consequence — don't change behavior. Either build the loop or don't build the goal.
Keeps — proven elements that earn their place
Keep

The 4 Essential Questions framework

The pedagogical spine of the series. Mirrors backward design principles and gives participants a consistent planning language across all three sessions.

Why it mattersShared vocabulary is the foundation of a learning culture. This framework earns its place in every session.
Keep

The "Lindsey" reteach activity

Analyzing a negative model and redesigning it is a cognitively rich task. Requires participants to evaluate, not just recall — a higher-order skill appropriate at this stage.

Why it mattersNegative examples are underused in adult learning. Seeing bad facilitation and having to fix it builds critical observation skills they'll use when they observe others.
Keep

"A Tale of Two Trainings" icebreaker

Best/worst training experience. The only icebreaker in the series that directly activates prior knowledge and connects to the session's content.

Why it mattersEvery other icebreaker is fun. This one is instructional. It earns its time by doing two jobs at once.
Changes — redesigned with research backing
Change

Filming + peer feedback → filming + guided self-review

Keep the filming. Drop unstructured peer feedback. Replace with a guided self-review protocol (focused questions, not open reflection), T&D-led focused observation on one dimension at a time, and a structured self-debrief (plus / delta / one thing) immediately after each round.

Why it mattersPeer feedback without feedback training produces hollow praise or vague criticism — neither changes behavior.
Research basis: Hattie & Timperley (2007) — feedback only improves performance when the receiver knows what "good" looks like. Ericsson's deliberate practice — narrowing feedback to one dimension accelerates growth. Zimmerman (2002) — self-generated next steps stick better than externally assigned ones.
Change

Capstone: original content creation → facilitating existing content

Assign a real piece of existing Bojangles content (e.g., a module from Own the Shift or a hospitality standard) and evaluate how participants bring it to life — their questioning, pacing, engagement design, and ability to make it feel relevant without drifting. Talking points remain as an annotation tool.

Why it mattersCreating your own content lets you hide behind preparation. Facilitating someone else's removes that safety net — you have to actually teach. The skill being certified is delivery, not authorship.
The conductor analogy: We don't certify a conductor by asking them to compose. We watch them lead an orchestra through music that already exists.
Change

Session 1 opening sequence — restructured

Currently jumps from appliance metaphor to case studies before participants have a shared vocabulary. Add explicit foundation first: what a facilitator is, how it differs from trainer/coach/mentor, and a worked example of the 4 Essential Questions before applying them.

Why it mattersCognitive load theory. You can't apply a framework you haven't fully internalized. Build the model first, then ask people to use it.
Change

Reflection architecture → Facilitator Growth Journal

Session 1 uses a 3-2-1. Session 2 uses goal-setting. Session 3 uses video + peer feedback. Three different formats with no common thread. Introduce a Facilitator Growth Journal — four consistent questions at the end of every session — so participants can see where they started.

Why it mattersGrowth requires a longitudinal artifact. Without one, participants can't track their own development and T&D can't demonstrate impact across the series.
Add new — structural gaps that need filling
Add

Pre-work before each session (15–30 min)

A reflection prompt before S1, a planning scenario before S2, and a soft skill outline before S3. Sessions currently start from zero. Pre-work turns the first hour of each session from catch-up into reinforcement.

Why it mattersSpaced practice research (Cepeda et al., 2006) shows that introducing concepts before a session dramatically increases in-session learning depth. Pre-exposure turns new content into reinforcement.
Add

Between-session checkpoints (required, not optional)

A Field Application Reflection (~30 days after S1) and a Peer Observation Task (before S3). These feed directly into the next session — not optional journaling, not self-paced, not skippable.

Why it mattersSix months between S1 and S3 with no accountability creates drift. Checkpoints convert a 3-event series into an ongoing development program.
Add

Certification rubric with observable standards

Five dimensions, observable criteria, three-outcome scoring: Certified, Conditionally Certified, Not Yet. Participants need to know what they're being evaluated against before the series begins.

Why it mattersWithout a standard, certification is a participation award. With one, it's a credential that means something. This is Build Priority #1.

Add

90-day post-certification field observation

A structured observation 90 days after certification, scored against a shortened rubric, documenting whether skills transferred to the field.

Why it mattersKirkpatrick Level 3. Did behavior change? You can't answer that without a mechanism to check. This closes the loop and generates the data needed for VP-level reporting.

Session 3 rationale

Two decisions. Both grounded in research.

01 Capstone shift

Facilitating existing content is harder than creating your own

When facilitators write their own content, they can design around their comfort zone — familiar examples, safe topics, material they know cold. Facilitating a pre-built module removes that safety net. They have to read the room, respond to what comes up, and make someone else's structure feel alive.

That's the actual job. The talking points work stays — it's how participants internalize and annotate existing content without drifting from it or reading it verbatim.

We don't certify a conductor by asking them to compose. We watch them lead an orchestra through music that already exists.
02 Feedback shift

Facilitation rounds replace one high-stakes capstone

Three or four short facilitation rounds (7–10 min each) with focused T&D observation beats one high-stakes filmed presentation on every dimension that matters: lower anxiety, higher practice volume, faster skill acquisition. After each round, the participant completes a structured self-debrief before receiving any external input.

Research basis: Rosenshine (2012) — high-frequency low-stakes practice accelerates skill acquisition. Ericsson — narrow focus per session outperforms comprehensive evaluation. Zimmerman (2002) — self-generated commitments produce more durable behavior change than externally assigned ones.
Measurement framework

Grounded in Kirkpatrick's Four Levels.

L1 Reaction

Did they value it?

End-of-session surveys enhanced with two items: confidence rating (pre/post) and one specific change suggestion. Trend tracked across cohorts to improve the design over time.

L2 Learning

Did they grow?

Facilitation rounds scored by T&D against the certification rubric — one dimension observed per round across multiple rounds. Final round is the certification decision point.

L3 Behavior

Did behavior change?

90-day field observation. Did the skills transfer to the real work? Scored on a shortened rubric by T&D or a certified peer observer. The most important data point in the stack.

L4 Results

Did it move the org?

Long-term: training consistency scores, new hire retention rates, and operational metrics in areas with certified facilitators vs. those without. The business case for the program.
