The framework

Teaching hides in leadership. Most leaders don't see it.

Every leader who shapes how others learn, decide, or change behavior is already a learning architect — whether they know it or not. The question is whether the design is intentional.

01

Teach

Design experiences that create understanding — not just information transfer. The best leaders don't explain more clearly. They architect moments where learning happens naturally, and they plan for transfer from the start.

Stop being the answer. Start being the architect. Facilitate, don't lecture — your role is to guide thinking, not deliver it.

02

Coach

Use strategic questions to unlock thinking rather than providing all the answers. Directive questions check for compliance. Guiding questions create understanding. One closes thinking. The other opens it.

Write guiding questions for the thinking level your audience is ready for — not the thinking level you wish they had.

03

Measure

Seek and confirm evidence of understanding through observable behavior — not assumption. Engagement makes us feel good. Evidence tells us if understanding actually happened. The two are not the same thing.

Define what "understanding" looks like as an observable behavior before the session starts — not after.

Key ideas

The thinking behind the session.

The dangerous assumption

Engagement is not evidence of understanding

We've all been there: people nodded, looked engaged, asked a question. You assume it landed. But engagement measures participation — not proficiency. And when we coach engagement, we miss whether learning actually transferred to behavior.

The question isn't "did they pay attention?" It's "what will they do differently tomorrow?"
The design discipline

Better thinking doesn't happen by accident

Think-Pair-Share isn't a warm-up — it's a deliberate design decision. Alone prevents groupthink. Pairs create safety to test ideas. Group builds collective knowledge. Every facilitation choice is an architecture choice.

Master Think-Pair-Share: give people time to think alone, then discuss in pairs, then share with the group. The sequence matters.
The transfer problem

Learning isn't the goal — transfer is

If a leader can't use a framework to design something they actually own, it's still just information. The culminating activity of any well-designed session forces the shift from "I understand it" to "I can apply it" — with a measurable indicator so you're not guessing based on vibes.

We're ending with application because that's where learning either happens or it doesn't.
The precision tool

Rewriting questions is where transfer happens

Sorting examples that are similar forces hierarchical comparison of nuances. Ordering forces decisions rooted in reality. Rewriting does two things: it protects the quality of thinking under pressure, and it ensures discussion moves toward the outcome actually needed — not whatever comes up naturally.

You're more likely to use questions you've made your own.
Design transparency

Metacognitive moments — the thinking behind the design.

We showed our work on purpose.

One of the distinctive design choices in this session was building in explicit "metacognitive moments" — brief pauses where the facilitation team named why the session was designed the way it was, so attendees could recognize and replicate the strategies themselves.

The goal wasn't just to deliver the framework — it was to model the framework in the act of delivering it. Every design decision in the session was itself a teaching demonstration.

This approach came directly from the same philosophy behind the Credible Feedback Process: the best way to develop a coach is to make your coaching thinking visible, not just your coaching behavior.

Metacognitive moment · Opening

Adults learn best when new ideas connect to prior knowledge. Starting with a reflection question surfaces assumptions before the framework is introduced — so learning has something real to attach to.

Metacognitive moment · Think-Pair-Share

We had you sort and select questions on purpose. Sorting similar examples forces hierarchical comparison of nuances that abstract explanation can't create.

Metacognitive moment · Closing

From the session

Building your question bank.

Guiding questions are one of the highest-leverage tools a leader has — designed to unlock thinking, not test for answers. Four types, each targeting a different level of cognitive engagement.

Connection questions

Bridge to prior knowledge

Create safe entry points. Surface what people already know so new ideas have something to attach to.

"How does this relate to what we discussed last week?"
"Where have you seen this work before?"
Analysis questions

Surface patterns and assumptions

Where the real thinking happens. Forces examination of what's actually driving a result.

"What patterns are you noticing?"
"What assumptions are we making?"
Application questions

Force transfer to real context

Bridges understanding to action. If someone can't answer this, learning hasn't transferred yet.

"How would you apply this tomorrow?"
"What's your next step?"
Reflection questions

Capture what shifted

Makes learning visible. If nothing shifted, it's worth knowing that before the session ends.

"What changed in your thinking?"
"What do you understand differently now?"
The 1–2–1 Rule

Before any session, prepare: 1 connection question (easy entry, builds safety) · 2 analysis questions (where the real thinking happens) · 1 application question (forces transfer). Four questions. Prepared in advance. Changes everything.
