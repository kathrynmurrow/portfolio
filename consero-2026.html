The conversation

What the panel covered.

01 The central question

Why does most learning not stick?

Organizations invest heavily in learning programs that produce completion rates — and little else. The panel examined what behavioral science actually tells us about retention, transfer, and behavior change: why information doesn't automatically become capability, and what it takes to close that gap by design.

02 The behavioral science angle

Designing for follow-through, not just attendance

The forum went deep on the psychology of retention — spaced repetition, retrieval practice, implementation intentions, and nudge theory. The practical implication: if behavior change isn't engineered into the design of a learning program, it won't emerge from attendance alone. Completion certificates don't produce behavior change. Deliberate follow-through architecture does.

03 The enterprise angle

Learning nudges at scale

Panelists shared specific applications from organizations operating at scale — where the challenge isn't designing one well-crafted learning experience, but engineering the conditions for behavior change across hundreds of sites and thousands of managers who are running operations while trying to develop their teams.

04 The Bojangles perspective

30-60-90 day follow-through as infrastructure

The automation pipeline being built at Bojangles — ILT events that trigger 30/60/90-day implementation reminders automatically — is a direct application of nudge theory at enterprise scale. The insight isn't that training needs to be better designed. It's that the environment after training needs to be deliberately engineered to reinforce what was learned.

Key themes

What came out of the discussion.

01 Retention science

Spacing beats massing

One well-designed four-hour training is less effective than four one-hour sessions spread over weeks. The research on spaced practice is unambiguous — and almost universally ignored in enterprise L&D because spaced delivery is harder to schedule and count.

The half-life of a training event is about 72 hours. Without deliberate reinforcement, most of what was learned has faded before the employee ever applies it.
02 Nudge design

Environment shapes behavior more than intention

Implementation intentions — "when X happens, I will do Y" — dramatically increase follow-through rates. Learning nudges are environmental cues designed to trigger those intentions at the right moment, in the right context, with minimal friction.

Don't ask managers to remember to coach. Engineer the moment when coaching is most likely to happen, and make it easy to act.
03 Transfer gap

Completion is the wrong metric

Organizations measure completion because it's easy to count. But completion tells you a person attended — it says nothing about whether behavior changed. Designing for transfer requires defining what observable behavior change looks like before the program is built, not after.

If you can't describe what "success" looks like as an observable behavior at 90 days, you're measuring the wrong thing.
The connection

The behavioral science framing from this panel is the intellectual foundation for the ILT communication pipeline being built at Bojangles — where every training event automatically triggers 30/60/90-day implementation reminders, classroom-to-field resources sent to supervisors, and follow-through nudges designed to close the gap that training alone never closes.
